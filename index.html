Proposal · Optics and neuroscience

Reality Labs × Chan Zuckerberg Biohub

I would like to propose a next generation miniature microscope for recording neural activity in freely behaving animals, built by translating the AR display engine and the miniature camera stack into a programmable fluorescence imaging system for neuroscience.

Harikumar Muthu, independent neurotechnology researcher. harineurotech.github.io/research-portfolio

I would like to propose a bounded Reality Labs and CZ Biohub feasibility collaboration: a next generation head-mounted neural miniscope, built by translating AR display-engine and miniature-camera capability into programmable fluorescence imaging of the brain.

A display engine emits light in an arbitrary spatiotemporal pattern at high frame rate, in a few cubic centimetres, on a power budget. Point that at brain tissue instead of at an eyebox and you get a microscope illuminator that no current miniscope has: light only where the cells are, and patterned optogenetic stimulation during imaging, in a freely moving animal.

Reality Labs has unusual depth in compact optics, image sensors, power and thermal co-design, calibration, and manufacturable reliability. Biohub has the neuroscience, the assays, and the instrument-validation rigour. Neither side builds this alone.

The ask is small: a short joint feasibility effort to define the biological requirements and retire the highest-risk optical, thermal, and motion-artifact questions before anyone commits to a program.

I carried this idea through my time at Reality Labs and I have not been able to put it down, quite honestly for the love of neuroscience. It is a rare chance to point world-class consumer-optics capability at a tool that would measurably change how we understand the brain.

01The idea in one paragraph

A display engine is an optical illumination system. That is the entire thesis, and everything below follows from it. In an AR headset the job is to place light of a chosen colour, at a chosen brightness, at a chosen pixel, at a chosen moment, inside a few cubic centimetres and on a battery. In a fluorescence miniscope the illuminator has exactly the same job, and in the limit an ideal miniscope illuminator is a display engine: arbitrary spatial pattern, arbitrary temporal sequence, tightly controlled wavelength and irradiance. Building a better illuminator on that definition improves neural imaging along three independent axes at once: miniaturisation, since size, mass, power and heat are the binding constraints on what a head-mounted instrument can do; arbitrary and structured illumination, since the pattern of light landing on tissue sets the background, the photobleaching, and the optical sectioning; and time multiplexing, since a fast programmable source lets one sensor and one optical path do the work of several. Each of those is expanded below.

03What a better illuminator buys the biology

Axis 01 · Miniaturisation

Every gram and milliwatt is a scientific constraint

Mass changes how the animal behaves; power becomes heat next to cortex; volume decides whether the instrument fits a mouse at all. These are the same constraints a consumer headset is designed against, and a display engine built to ship in one already carries the answers: high wall-plug-efficiency emitters, integrated drivers, thermal paths that hold up on a body. Transplanting that consumer architecture into biology buys longer sessions, smaller animals, and more natural behaviour, with a tissue-adjacent thermal budget verified the way a skin-contact budget already is.

Axis 02 · Arbitrary patterns

Light only where the cells are

Uniform flood illumination excites tissue you are not recording, and that light comes back as background and as photobleaching. Illuminating only the resolved somata cuts both without touching signal, and candidate architectures in public including LCoS, micro-LED arrays, and MEMS optical scanning and so on can support spatially addressable illumination. The same capability gives structured illumination for optical sectioning, and patterned optogenetic stimulation during imaging, which today exists only on a benchtop with a head-fixed animal.

SNR ≈ Ns / √(Ns + Nb + npσr²) Everyone optimises the signal term Ns. A patterned source is the only way to attack the background term Nb.
Axis 03 · Time multiplexing

One optical path, several measurements

A source that switches faster than the indicator can move lets you interleave wavelengths frame by frame for multicolour or ratiometric imaging on a single sensor, alternate imaging and stimulation frames so stimulation light never lands in an exposure, and modulate the source for lock-in rejection of ambient and autofluorescence background.

06How the work would run

  1. Define the measurementIndicator, brain region, depth, behaviour, field of view, spatial sampling, temporal bandwidth, acceptable mass and tether. Biology sets the specification and optics follows it.
  2. Close the budgets on a breadboardPhoton and thermal budgets, excitation uniformity and patterning, collection efficiency, filter leakage, sensor noise, motion sensitivity, all measured before anything is miniaturised.
  3. Miniaturise and benchmarkHead to head against an established miniscope on metrics agreed in advance: mass, tissue-adjacent temperature rise, power, field of view, resolution, frame rate, SNR, motion robustness, calibration drift, unit-to-unit spread.

07The ask

An FRO-like moonshot project, inside the two institutions that already have the pieces

Something shaped like a focused research organisation is right for this, at small scale: a compact fully-resourced team, a fixed horizon, one deliverable that neither academia nor a product roadmap will produce on its own, and a hard go or no-go at the end. This one is unusually cheap because neither side has to build a new capability, only to point existing ones at a new target.

Reality Labs contributes the craft of making difficult optical systems disappear into reliable hardware. Biohub contributes the neuroscience that decides whether each engineering choice earns its place. The output is an open, reproducible instrument that the whole field can build on, which is exactly the kind of thing the Biohub exists to fund and exactly the kind of thing a consumer optics org never gets to make.

Concretely: a handful of Reality Labs optical, sensor and thermal engineers alongside a Biohub imaging group, for a feasibility phase, ending in a demonstrated breadboard and a specification for the miniaturised instrument.
